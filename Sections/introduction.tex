Quantum optics classifies light sources based on the photon emission statistics they exhibit. 
Phase-coherent sources, like lasers, emit independent photons with low intensity fluctuations. 
The behaviour of this stream of coherent photons is accounted by \textbf{Poissonian statistics}. 
In contrast, thermal sources have larger intensity fluctuations due to the random atomic processes that generate the stream. 
Thus, this is the regime is modeled with \textbf{super-Poissonian statistics}.
%The bosonic nature of photons in these sources manifests in a phenomenon called photon bunching. 
%Because these photons tend to occupy the same spatiotemporal modes, they arrive in clumps separated by dark lulls. 
The third category is reserved for quantum, single-photon sources. 
In this regime, photons arrive individually, leading to an effect known as photon anti-bunching. 
Intensity fluctuations of quantum sources are captured by \textbf{sub-poissonian statistics}.  

The focus of this work is to measure the statistical properties of these distinct light sources. 
This is achieved using state-of-the-art single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs) and time taggers, which enable the recording of individual photon arrivals and the measurement of coincidences between two SPAD channels. 
To accurately extract the pure statistics of the light sources, the hardware is first be rigorously baselined. 
Specifically, the four primary performance indicators of the SPADs —jitter, photon detection probability (PDP), dark count rate (DCR), and dead time— are quantified using Time-Correlated Single Photon Counting (TCSPC) techniques. 
With the sensor fully characterized, the setup is then utilized to perform high-resolution coincidence measurements across the different light sources, allowing us to accurately report their intrinsic statistical properties while mathematically accounting for detector artifacts.


\subsection{SPAD Performance Indicators}
Inside SPADs, a photon incident of the active area of a SPAD detector, triggers an avalanche of charge carriers creating a current signal. 
The current is later detected and converted to a digital signal; typicaly within less than $100 ps$. 
Good SPAD detectors have low jitter, deadtimes and dark count rates (DCR) while mantaining high photon detection probabilities (PDP). \\

\textbf{Jitter} refers to the temporal delay between the time of photon arrival and signal detection.
Timing jitter is quantified by measuring delay $\Delta t$ between the emission of 
a LASER pulse (recorded by a Laser Clock), and the detection signal from the SPAD. 
Accumulating these events in a histogram it is possible to extract the instrument response function (IRF). 
The jitter is then reported as the Full Width at Half Maximum (FWHM) of this distribution. \\

After an avalanche is triggered, the SPAD must reduce the voltage across the diode to stop the current in a process called quenching. 
A period of voltage recharge immediately follows to restore the detector ability to detect incoming photons.  
The time period between the start of the avalanche and the end of the recharge is referred to as \textbf{dead-time} because the detector remains blind to subsequent incident photons. \\

Not every incident photon successfully triggers a macroscopic avalanche. 
\textbf{PDP} quantifies the percentage of incident photons that result in a measurable electrical pulse.
This indicator is dependent on the photon's wavelength and the detector's overvoltage. \\

SPADs will occasionally register false counts even in complete darkness. 
\textbf{DCR} counts are mainly triggered by thermally generated charge carriers inside the depletion region that initiate an avalanche indistinguishable from a true photon detection.
Tunneling effects and lattice defects are also causes of DCR. \\



\subsection{Statistical Properties of Light}
\textbf{Phase-coherent sources} are defined by an electromagnetic field that oscillates with a precise, constant phase over time. 
A popular quantum model for these systems makes use of the second quantization formalism of the harmonic oscillator, resulting in a coherent state $|\alpha\rangle$. 
This state is the right eigenstate of the photon annihilation operator $\hat{a}$, such that $\hat{a}|\alpha\rangle = \alpha|\alpha\rangle$. 
The eigenvalue $\alpha$ is a complex number where its squared amplitude encodes the average number of photons ($|\alpha|^2 = \mu$), and its angle represents the macroscopic phase of the field.\\

To calculate the probability of detecting $n$ photons, a projection of the coherent state onto the photon number \footnote{Also called Fock basis.} basis results in
\begin{equation}
|\alpha\rangle = e^{-\frac{|\alpha|^2}{2}} \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{\alpha^n}{\sqrt{n!}} |n\rangle.
\end{equation}
The probability $P(n) = |\langle n | \alpha \rangle|^2$ simplifies exactly to a Poissonian distribution. 
Because the phase is stable, individual photons behave completely independently; the arrival of one photon provides no predictive information about the next. 
Consequently, the variance equals the mean ($\sigma^2 = \mu$). \\

\textbf{Non-coherent sources} —such as stars, flames, or continuous lasers passed through rotating diffusers—exhibit super-Poissonian statistics. 
In this regime, the total electric field at the detector is the superposition of countless independent waves with random, fluctuating phases:
\begin{equation}
E(t) = \sum_i E_i e^{-i(\omega t + \phi_i(t))}
\end{equation}

Because $E(t)$ is the sum of many random variables, the Central Limit Theorem dictates that the electric field becomes a Gaussian random variable. 
The absolute square of a Gaussian random field yields a macroscopic intensity $I(t)$ that strictly follows an exponential probability distribution:
\begin{equation}
P(I) = \frac{1}{\langle I \rangle} e^{-\frac{I}{\langle I \rangle}}.
\end{equation}

The bosonic nature of photons in these chaotic fields manifests in a phenomenon called photon bunching. 
Because photons prefer to occupy the same modes, they arrive in clumps followed by dark lulls. 
This broadens the photon number distribution into a Bose-Einstein distribution, significantly increasing the statistical noise such that $\sigma^2 = \mu + \mu^2$.\\

\textbf{Single-photon sources} belong to a strictly quantum regime that cannot be described by classical waves. 
Because the source physically emits photons one at a time, the probability of detecting two photons simultaneously is zero. 
This forced temporal separation leads to photon anti-bunching, producing a statistical distribution that is tighter than a coherent laser, characterized by $\sigma^2 < \mu$.

\subsubsection{Second Order Coherence $g^{(2)}$}
The second order correlation function $g^{(2)}$ quantifies intensity correlations, photon statistics and time separated probability of detecting two photons. 
For reference, a value for $g^{(2)}(0) < 1$, is an indicator of antibuching; a quantum characteristic of the light source.
In contrast, an ideal coherent state yields $g^{(2)}(0) = 1$. While thermal light gives $ g^{(2)}(0) = 2 $. \\

Tu summarize, second order coherence serves as a tool that dishtinguishes the nature of a variety of light sources. 
This work aims to present the reader a experimental procedure that measures $g^{(2)}$, leveraging the power of SPAD and time tagger technology.
