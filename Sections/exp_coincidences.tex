\subsection{1st Measurement: HBT}
\label{subsec:CorrVal}
First measurements measure second order coherence at $\tau = 0$ i.e $g^{(2)}(0)$. The procedure is given bellow. 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Feed the Pulsed Laser into the optical setup in figure \ref{fig:exp_lay}. 
    \item Select a Laser frequency: $1/T_{dead} \approx 2\cdot10^8 = 200 MHz$  . 
    \item Select Exposure Time. 
    \item Connect Laser Sync and SPAD channels into the time tagger. 
    \item Measure timestamps of detection events. 
    \item Compare Channel Ticks. 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item[]  $\Delta t_{coincidence} = \Delta t_{B} - \Delta t_{A} $
        \item[] Coincidence if $\Delta t_{coincidence} \leq 2 \cdot Jitter$
    \end{itemize}
    \item Calculate Probabilities 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item[] $P_{A|B} = \frac{\# Events_{A|B}}{\# Pulses}$
        \item[] $P_{AB} = \frac{\# Coincidences}{\# Pulses}$
    \end{itemize}
    \item Calculate Correlation 
    \begin{itemize}
        \item[] $g^{(2)}(0) = \frac{P_{AB}}{P_{A} \cdot P_{B}} $
    \end{itemize}
    \item Check 3, 4 fold correlations $g^{(3)}, g^{(4)}, ...$
\end{enumerate}

This measurement configuration---cross-correlating the digital timestamps from two independent SPAD pixels to extract the intensity fluctuations---operates as a Hanbury Brown and Twiss (HBT) intensity interferometer \cite{hanbury1956correlation}. 
By utilizing two distinct spatial channels, the setup measures coincident photon arrivals at zero temporal delay ($g^{(2)}(0)$).

\subsection{2nd Measurement: $g^{(2)}(\tau)$}
Second measurements designed to extract the second order cohrence function $g^{(2)}(\tau)$.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Feed the Pulsed Laser into the optical setup in figure \ref{fig:exp_lay}. 
    \item Select a Laser frequency: $1/T_{dead} \approx 2\cdot10^8 = 200 MHz$.
    \item Build Histogram of $G^{(2)}(\tau)$
    \item Normalize histogram by the average count of the time bins far from $\tau  = 0$ to get $g^{(2)}(\tau)$. 
\end{enumerate}

For the Pulsed Laser we expect to see two behaviours depending on the programed binwidth: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item If \textit{binwidth} is matches the period of the pulsed laser we expect $g^{(2)}(0) = 1$.
    \item If \textit{binwidth} is shorter than the period of the pulsed laser, we expect a comb structure.
\end{itemize}

In contrast, for thermal sources, $1 < g^{(2)}(0) \leq 2$ and decays over coherence times. 
The width of this decay is a measure of the coherence time \footnote{Set by the diffuser speed}.

This issue arises because the digital bin width is centered arround 0 (i.e $\tau_{bin0} = [-1/2f_{Laser}, +1/2f_{Laser}]$ rather than $\tau_{bin0} = [0, +1/f_{Laser}]$ ).
To resolve this bin-edge quantization error, an artificial hardware offset equal to exactly half the bin width was introduced via the input delays. 
This offset physically shifts the photon arrival times such that the coincidence peak lands dead-center inside Bin 0, safely away from the edges. 
The corrected measurement, successfully eliminating the binning artifacts, is presented in Figure \ref{fig:g2(tau)_measurement_corrected_final}.

\subsection{Third Measurement Plan: Diffuse Correlation Spectroscopy}

As seen in the second measurement, true thermal light decorrelates in femtoseconds, which is too fast for the SPAD dead-time to measure. To solve this, we built a ``pseudothermal'' light source to successfully measure photon bunching. We shined a 785 nm continuous-wave (CW) laser through a rotating diffuser to create a moving speckle pattern. By changing the motor speed, we artificially slow down the light's intensity fluctuations to the millisecond regime, allowing our Time Tagger to capture it.

The measurement procedure was as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A 785 nm CW laser was aligned to hit the outer edge of a rotating diffuser.
    \item The linear SPAD array was placed 0.5 cm behind the diffuser to detect the speckles.
    \item To avoid hardware buffer overflow from high count rates, we lowered the laser intensity to a safe kilocounts-per-second (kcps) level.
    \item To recover the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR), we simultaneously measured the autocorrelation of 11 separate pixels to average them together.
    \item The measurement was repeated for different diffuser angular velocities (5, 10, 15, and 20 deg/s).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Results} \\
To process the data, each pixel's measurement was normalized by dividing it by its flat baseline. Figure \ref{fig:dcs_average_fit} shows the 11 individual pixels (faded) and their combined average (black). Averaging the pixels gives a much smoother exponential decay curve, allowing us to accurately fit the data and extract the decorrelation time ($\tau_c$). 

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/average11_decorrelation_spectroscopy.png}
    \caption{Normalized autocorrelation measurement for the rotating diffuser. The black line is the average of 11 pixels, which is fitted with an exponential curve (red dashed line) to extract $\tau_c$.}
    \label{fig:dcs_average_fit}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:spatial_averaging} demonstrates why using the 11-pixel array is so powerful. As we average more pixels ($N$), the measured decorrelation time remains stable, but the baseline noise drops follows the theoretical $1/\sqrt{N}$ rule. 

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/averageeffect_decorrelation_spectroscopy.png}
    \caption{Effect of averaging multiple pixels. The measured $\tau_c$ remains stable (Left), while the noise drops with $1/\sqrt{N}$ (Right).}
    \label{fig:spatial_averaging}
\end{figure}

Finally, Figure \ref{fig:velocity_sweep} plots the extracted $\tau_c$ against the different motor speeds. As the diffuser spins faster, the speckles move past the detector faster, causing the decorrelation time to drop. This inverse relationship successfully validates our Diffuse Correlation Spectroscopy measurement.
